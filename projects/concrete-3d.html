Carbon Sequestration in 3D-Printed Houses

Collaboration with SQ4D to reduce carbon impact of 3D-printed housing

Setting: August 2021 - May 2022; Capstone project with Yale University and SQ4D on a team of four

Summary

The world is facing a polycrisis of climate change and housing shortages. Concrete usage represents a significant proportion of the total carbon dioxide emissions globally, but can be used in modified 3D printers to cheaply and quickly construct housing. For this project, I worked on a team of four to help company SQ4D reduce their carbon emissions through carbon sequestration and reduced cement usage in concrete. Through a variety of approaches, we managed to reduce construction costs by ~$3000 and reduce CO2 by two tons per single family house.

Problem

3D printed houses are environmentally harmful but have the potential to scale housing development rapidly. 3D printing construction companies need a cleaner concrete mix to positively contribute to both challenges.

Solution

Carbon is emitted through cement production before cement is mixed with aggregate to form concrete. To reduce carbon intensity of housing production, cement usage must be reduced or significant carbon must be entrained in cement.

We investigated many modes of entraining carbon dioxide and settled on several approaches:

  1. Use a custom nozzle to aerate concrete with gaseous CO2. Cement reacts with the gas, resulting in faster curing and stored CO2. To prevent jamming from accelerated curing, need to redesign nozzle to allow for easy cleaning and aeration as opposed to applying CO2 upstream.
  2. Replace some cement with baking soda. Calcium carbonate can be created through processes that trap carbon dioxide, making it a potential material to sequester carbon. Replacing cement results in a less carbon-intensive product, but again influences curing time and strength.
  3. Add entrainment fluid to concrete, which produces air bubbles to reduce concrete density (thereby reducing the material cost).
  4. Replace sand with char, soot, and concrete pellets instead of sand for aggregate.
  5. To preserve bubble size, we minimized tamping, a process that improves concrete strength by making the material more homogeneous.

Each of these solutions led to a cleaner product that resulted in two tons of carbon savings and over $3000 saved in material costs.

My Contribution

I performed all materials science research to understand bubble formation in concrete as well as curing behavior when exposed to carbon dioxide. I designed all experiments and performed all analysis for the team of four.
