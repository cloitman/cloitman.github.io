Downscaling California Load for Capacity Expansion

Placing statewide load projections at real substations and on the buses of a nodal California grid model

May 2026 – Present • Energy Modeling, Analysis, and Control Group, UC Berkeley

Overview

Capacity expansion models of California increasingly run on detailed nodal networks, but the load they are given is rarely resolved below the balancing-authority or regional level. I built an open-source pipeline that takes statewide and regional load projections (the CEC's Integrated Energy Policy Report through 2050, the CPUC's RESOLVE model, and ReEDS), together with EIA-930 historical demand, and places them at real utility substations and on the buses of the California Test System (CATS; Taylor et al., IEEE Transactions on Energy Markets, Policy and Regulation, 2024). I am the sole developer, and the code and documentation are public: github.com/Energy-MAC/california-hourly-disaggregation.

Motivation

Planning a power grid is a long-horizon problem: transmission lines take years to permit and build, and decisions made today lock in infrastructure for decades. California is a particularly high-stakes case — load patterns are being reshaped by electric vehicles, growing air conditioning use, and rooftop solar, which suppresses net demand during the day and steepens evening ramps.

Public projections give statewide or regional totals, but a nodal planning model needs to know which bus carries that load, hour by hour. This project is not a forecast: it takes a projection someone else has made and answers where on the network that load sits.

Data

I assembled a dataset of 1,347 PG&E, SCE, and SDG&E substations with coordinates, voltages, distributed-resource attributes, and month-by-hour load ranges, scraping and reconciling about ten public sources: utility distribution-planning data, the CEC's statewide substation inventory, EIA-930 and EIA-861, IEPR, RESOLVE, and ReEDS. Cross-referencing showed that all 3,171 CATS substation buses match a record in the CEC's 2026 inventory within 2 km, so that inventory can serve as CATS's coordinate source. I revised and documented the rules for matching substations across sources.

Methods

Every method conserves the statewide total exactly in every hour, so differences between them come only from where load is placed.

  • Proportional weights: each substation receives its share of its region's load in each month and hour, through either a county-level chain (ReEDS) or a utility-level chain (IEPR, RESOLVE).
  • Stochastic model: Monte Carlo draws that reproduce each substation's measured load range while moving with the statewide total through a common factor. Validated on EIA-930 data from 2015–2025: median error in recovering substation load ranges of 1.1%, and hourly tracking error of 0.40%.
  • Coordinate-free allocation: for node systems that come without coordinates, allocating in proportion to the levels supplied for each node.
  • Mapping to buses: nearest-bus assignment, identity matching through CEC records (80.6% of substations matched to the bus built from their own record), and catchments solved as a transportation linear program.

A scikit-learn side study tried to predict substation load from structural features. It recovered a substation's load shape but not its magnitude, which is why predicted profiles were never used in the pipeline.

Use and Status

Colleagues are using packaged outputs from the pipeline — hourly loads at CATS buses for RESOLVE's 2035 forecast, and a grid of 160 model-year and weather-year scenarios — in capacity expansion studies, one on environmental justice and one comparing transmission and storage investment. I am also lead author of a working paper on one part of this work: how the method used to place statewide load on CATS's buses changes dispatch, prices, and whether the network can deliver the load. Results will be added here as the paper advances.
