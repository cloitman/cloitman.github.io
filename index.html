About Me

I work on how batteries, heat pumps, and smart thermostats can be operated as part of the power system: bid into markets, dispatched within network constraints, and coordinated so that a renewable-heavy grid needs less new transmission and generation.

I'm a Quantitative Researcher at Tyba Energy, where I build battery-plus-solar co-optimization strategies in CAISO and bidding models in ERCOT and MISO. I'm also developing a risk-sharing model that lets storage and renewable operators trade with each other to reduce variance in returns. Separately, I'm writing a first-author paper from my research with Duncan Callaway's Energy Modeling, Analysis, and Control Group at UC Berkeley. There I built an open-source pipeline that places California's long-term load projections at real substations and on the buses of a statewide grid model, and colleagues now use its outputs in studies of environmental justice and of transmission and storage investment. The paper asks how much the way load is placed on that grid changes what the model concludes.

I came to power systems from engineering and economics. At Yale I paired mechanical engineering with resource economics and game theory, then spent two years at Flow Traders, where I expanded the desk's energy strategies and built the firm's first carbon credit trading strategies. I returned to school for a Master of Climate Solutions at UC Berkeley. There, two course projects sited and dispatched batteries on the same distribution feeder, once for a social planner and once for a profit-maximizing operator. I want to study how to improve each approach in a power grid increasingly dominated with renewables.

I hold a B.S. in Mechanical Engineering and Economics from Yale University, where I received the L.C. Lichty and E.O. Waters Prize in Mechanical Engineering.

Featured Project

Virtual Power Plant Research

Battery allocation, arbitrage optimization, and residential storage deployment strategy — CEE295, ENERES 254, and an independent study with SunStrong Management

Two course research projects develop convex optimization (CVXPY) frameworks for battery siting and arbitrage profit maximization on a 13-node IEEE radial feeder, using real CAISO day-ahead market LMP data. A third project, an independent study, applies this work — alongside financial modeling and California policy research — to produce deployment recommendations for SunStrong Management, which leases more than 10,000 residential solar systems. Key findings: a social planner concentrates storage at the feeder’s bottleneck node, while price-taking operators spread it across low-cost nodes; the planner’s benefit levels off near 30 MWh and operator profit flattens above about 22 MWh; and SDG&E territory offers the best risk-adjusted returns for residential battery rollout.

Python · CVXPY · Optimal Power Flow · Battery Storage · CAISO Data · Policy Research

About Me

I'm an energy systems researcher with a background in mechanical engineering, economics, and quantitative trading. I'm interested in the decisions that let distributed resources reinforce grid infrastructure: how batteries and flexible loads should bid, dispatch, and coordinate when prices and network constraints are both uncertain.

I finished a Master of Climate Solutions at UC Berkeley in August 2026. I now work as a Quantitative Researcher at Tyba Energy, where I manage dispatch for battery and solar portfolios, and I'm writing up my research from the Energy Modeling, Analysis, and Control Group at UC Berkeley as a first-author working paper.

Longer term, I want to work on the controls and market design that let virtual power plants and other fleets of distributed resources deliver grid value reliably.

Background

I grew up in St. Louis, MO and am a proud fan of the Cardinals and the Blues. I love the Midwest and will happily tell everyone about the wonderful museums and great food back home. I spent most of the summers when I was younger canoeing in the Boundary Waters Canoe Area in Northern Minnesota which gave me a passion for nature and conservation.

Starting in middle school, I was an active member of Science Olympiad and decided early on in high school I would become an engineer to contribute to renewables to combine my interests in sustainability and science. At Yale, I chose mechanical engineering to learn about the engineering opportunities and constraints of energy systems and contributed to materials science research to support solar panels, which was where I first became exposed to data science.

Hoping to learn about the incentives that allow the proliferation of fossil fuels, I decided to take a game theory class. I loved the mathematical modelling aspects of the subject and decided to add an economics major, where I also studied resource economics. During COVID, instead of a remote semester, I did mathematical finance research at Ellington Management Group, a hedge fund, under the supervision of a Yale mathematical economics professor. I built calibration algorithms to recover probability densities from credit default swaption prices.

Still passionate about energy, and drawn to the quantitative methods I had learned at Ellington, I joined Flow Traders as an index ETF trader and moved to the commodities desk, where I helped automate the desk's futures-based ETF trading and expanded its energy strategies and built the firm's first carbon credit trading strategies. I enjoyed the work, but I wanted it to have a more direct environmental impact and to use more of my engineering training, so I returned to school at UC Berkeley. There I found that power systems modelling combines the parts of engineering and economics I like best: physical constraints, optimization, and the incentives of the people who operate the system.

What I'm Working On

At Tyba Energy, I build battery and solar co-optimization strategies in CAISO and real-time and ancillary-service bidding models in ERCOT and MISO. I'm also developing a risk-sharing model that lets battery and renewable operators trade with each other to reduce variance in returns. Beyond Tyba, I'm preparing a first-author working paper on how the way load is disaggregated onto a nodal California grid model changes its results, and I'm interested in how storage and controls can make virtual power plants dependable grid resources.
