About Me

I work on how batteries, heat pumps, and smart thermostats can be operated as part of the power system: bid into markets, dispatched within network constraints, and coordinated so that a renewable-heavy grid needs less new transmission and generation.

I'm a Quantitative Researcher at Tyba Energy, where I build battery-plus-solar co-optimization strategies in CAISO and bidding models in ERCOT and MISO. I'm also developing a risk-sharing model that lets storage and renewable operators trade with each other to reduce variance in returns. Separately, I'm writing a first-author paper from my research with Duncan Callaway's Energy Modelling, Analysis, and Control Group at UC Berkeley. There I downscaled California's long-term load projections to the nodes of a statewide grid model and compared transmission and storage investment; the paper asks how much the way load is placed on that grid changes what the model concludes.

I came to power systems from engineering and economics. At Yale I paired mechanical engineering with resource economics and game theory, then spent two years at Flow Traders, where I built the desk's energy strategies and the firm's first carbon credit trading strategies. I returned to school for a Master of Climate Solutions at UC Berkeley. There, two course projects sited and dispatched batteries on the same distribution feeder, once for a social planner and once for a profit-maximizing operator. I want to study how to improve each approach in a power grid increasingly dominated with renewables.

I hold a B.S. in Mechanical Engineering and a B.A. in Economics from Yale University, where I received the L.C. Lichty and E.O. Waters Prize in Mechanical Engineering.

Virtual Power Plant Research

Battery allocation, arbitrage optimization, and residential storage deployment strategy — CEE295, ENERES 254, and an independent study with SunStrong Management

Two course research projects develop convex optimization (CVXPY) frameworks for battery siting and arbitrage profit maximization on a 13-node IEEE radial feeder, using real CAISO day-ahead market LMP data. A third project, an independent study, applies this work — alongside financial modeling and California policy research — to produce deployment recommendations for SunStrong Management, which leases approximately 10,000 residential solar systems. Key findings: battery siting converges to the most congested network node; optimal capacity saturates around 22–28 MWh; and SDG&E territory offers the best risk-adjusted returns for residential battery rollout.

About Me

I'm an energy systems researcher with a background in mechanical engineering, economics, and quantitative trading. I'm interested in the decisions that let distributed resources reinforce grid infrastructure: how batteries and flexible loads should bid, dispatch, and coordinate when prices and network constraints are both uncertain.

I finished a Master of Climate Solutions at UC Berkeley in August 2026. I now work as a Quantitative Researcher at Tyba Energy, where I research and operate battery and solar portfolios, and I'm writing up my research from the Energy Modelling, Analysis, and Control Group at UC Berkeley as a first-author working paper.

Longer term, I want to work on the controls and market design that let virtual power plants and other fleets of distributed resources deliver grid value reliably.

Background

I grew up in St. Louis, MO and am a proud fan of the Cardinals and the Blues. I love the Midwest and will happily tell everyone about the wonderful museums and great food back home. I spent most of the summers when I was younger canoeing in the Boundary Waters Canoe Area in Northern Minnesota which gave me a passion for nature and conservation.

Starting in middle school, I was an active member of Science Olympiad and decided early on in high school I would become an engineer to contribute to renewables to combine my interests in sustainability and science. At Yale, I chose mechanical engineering to learn about the engineering opportunities and constraints of energy systems and contributed to materials science research to support solar panels, which was where I first became exposed to data science.

Hoping to learn about the incentives that allow the proliferation of fossil fuels, I decided to take a game theory class. I loved the mathematical modelling aspects of the subject and decided to add an economics degree, where I also studied resource economics. During COVID, instead of a remote semester, I did mathematical finance research at Ellington Management Group, a hedge fund, under the supervision of a Yale mathematical economics professor. I built calibration algorithms to recover probability densities from credit default swaption prices.

Still passionate about energy, and drawn to the quantitative methods I had learned at Ellington, I joined Flow Traders as a commodities trader. I helped automate the desk's futures-based ETF trading and built its energy strategies and the firm's first carbon credit trading strategies. I enjoyed the work, but I wanted it to have a more direct environmental impact and to use more of my engineering training, so I returned to school at UC Berkeley. There I found that power systems modelling combines the parts of engineering and economics I like best: physical constraints, optimization, and the incentives of the people who operate the system.

What I'm Working On

At Tyba Energy, I build battery and solar co-optimization strategies in CAISO and real-time and ancillary-service bidding models in ERCOT and MISO. I'm also developing a risk-sharing model that lets battery and renewable operators trade with each other to reduce variance in returns. Beyond Tyba, I'm preparing a first-author working paper on how the way load is disaggregated onto a nodal California grid model changes its results, and I'm interested in how storage and controls can make virtual power plants dependable grid resources.
